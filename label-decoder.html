How Ingredient Lists Work

Under FSSAI regulations, ingredients must be listed in descending order by weight at the time of manufacture. This means the first ingredient is the most abundant. If sugar appears in the first three slots — even split across multiple aliases — it's a significant component of that product.

Key rule: Water counts as an ingredient when it's added intentionally. Compound ingredients (e.g., "chocolate coating") must also break down their own sub-ingredients in parentheses if they constitute more than 2% of the finished product.

What to Look For

  • If whole grain is claimed, it should appear as the first ingredient, not a secondary one
  • Watch for "enriched flour" — this is refined flour with a few vitamins added back after stripping
  • "Natural flavour" is a legal term that can cover hundreds of chemical compounds
  • Compounds ending in -ose (maltose, dextrose, galactose) are sugars
  • Multiple small quantities of the same ingredient split under different aliases is a technique to push each one further down the list

The 14 Major Allergens Regulated by FSSAI

FSSAI's Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations 2020 mandate that the following 14 allergens must be declared in bold, contrasting font, and ideally in a separate "Contains:" statement:

🌾 Gluten-containing cereals (Wheat, Rye, Barley, Oats) 🦐 Crustaceans & shellfish 🥚 Eggs 🐟 Fish 🥜 Peanuts 🫘 Soybeans 🥛 Milk (including lactose) 🌰 Tree nuts (Almonds, Cashews, Walnuts, etc.) 🌱 Celery 🌿 Mustard 🫘 Sesame seeds 🧴 Sulphur dioxide & Sulphites (>10 mg/kg) 🦑 Molluscs 🫐 Lupin

How Allergens Appear on Labels

  • In the ingredient list: The allergen source is printed in bold each time it appears — e.g., "Wheat flour, Milk powder, Soy lecithin [Soy]"
  • "Contains" statement: A dedicated line such as "Contains: Wheat, Milk, Soy" appearing after or near the ingredient list
  • May Contain / Cross-contamination: Voluntary advisory statements like "May contain traces of Peanuts" — not legally mandated but increasingly common for shared-facility products
ℹ️
"May Contain" is advisory, not guaranteed. It means the product is manufactured in a facility or on equipment that also processes the named allergen. The risk varies — some factories have excellent cleaning protocols; others do not. Individuals with severe allergies should contact the manufacturer directly.

Hidden Allergen Sources

  • Milk: Whey, casein, lactalbumin, lactoglobulin, ghee, butter, cream, curd
  • Wheat/Gluten: Atta, maida, semolina (suji/rava), starch (if wheat-derived), malt extract
  • Soy: Soya sauce, textured vegetable protein (TVP), tofu, edamame, miso, lecithin
  • Peanuts: Groundnut oil (unless highly refined), mixed nuts, some pastes
  • Fish: Worcestershire sauce, Caesar dressing, some Omega-3 supplements
  • Sulphites: Dried fruits, wine, vinegar, some jams and pickles

What to Do If an Allergen is Missing from a Label

If you suspect an allergen is present but undeclared, you can report the product to FSSAI via the FOSCOS portal or the 1800-112-100 consumer helpline.

Serving Size vs. Package Size

Nutrition information on Indian labels is declared per 100 g (or 100 ml) AND per serving. The serving size is defined by the manufacturer — and it's often much smaller than the amount a person actually eats in one sitting. This is a legal requirement under FSSAI 2020 regulations.

⚠️ Watch out: A "serving" of chips might be listed as 30 g, but a typical packet is 150 g. If you eat the whole packet, you've consumed 5 servings — multiply all nutrition values by 5.

How to Calculate Your Actual Intake

  1. 1
    Find the serving size Located near the top of the nutrition table. Usually in grams or millilitres. Sometimes also expressed as "1 biscuit (12 g)" or "2 tablespoons (30 g)".
  2. 2
    Find servings per package Also near the top. If it says "Servings: 5" and the pack is 150 g, each serving is 30 g.
  3. 3
    Weigh or estimate what you actually eat Use a kitchen scale for accuracy, or estimate based on pieces/volume consumed.
  4. 4
    Scale the nutrition values Multiply each value by (amount you ate ÷ serving size). Or use the per-100 g column for easier maths.

📐 Worked Example: Instant Noodles

Label says: Serving size 75 g (½ cake) · Servings per pack: 2 · Sodium per serving: 820 mg

You eat: 1 full cake (150 g = 2 servings)

Actual sodium intake = 820 mg × 2 = 1,640 mg
FSSAI daily adequate intake = 2,000 mg/day
That's 82% of your daily sodium in one meal!

The label's per-serving figure (820 mg) sounds reasonable until you account for eating the full packet.

Tips for Smarter Reading

  • Always check the per 100 g column for apples-to-apples comparison between products
  • If a product claims "only 80 kcal per serving," verify how large that serving is
  • Snack packs often use very small serving sizes — a single biscuit instead of the 3–4 most people eat
  • For drinks, serving sizes may be 200 ml when a standard bottle is 500 ml

What are E-Numbers and INS Codes?

Food additives are assigned E-numbers in the European system or INS (International Numbering System) codes by Codex Alimentarius. In India, FSSAI adopts INS codes — they're identical numbers to E-codes. "E621" and "INS 621" refer to the same substance (MSG). All permitted additives must appear in the ingredient list with their INS number or name.

Not all additives are harmful. Many have centuries of safe use (E440 = pectin from fruit peels; E500 = baking soda). The key is understanding what each does and whether you're consuming it in large quantities.

Common E-Numbers on Indian Labels

Code Name Function Safety Notes

E122 / INS 122 Azorubine (Carmoisine) Red colouring ⚠ Caution — Azo dye; Southampton Six member; may cause reactions in aspirin-sensitive individuals
E124 / INS 124 Ponceau 4R Red colouring ⚠ Caution — Azo dye; Southampton Six; banned in USA and Norway
E129 / INS 129 Allura Red AC Red colouring (confectionery, drinks) ⚠ Caution — Southampton Six; proposed ban in EU under consideration as of 2023

How to Read Additive Declarations

  • Indian labels use the format: Function [INS number] — e.g., "Antioxidant [INS 307b]" or "Colour [INS 102]"
  • Multiple additives of the same function are comma-separated: "Preservatives [INS 211, INS 202]"
  • Natural and nature-identical flavours don't need individual INS codes — they may simply say "Natural flavour" or "Artificial flavour"
  • Additives in compound ingredients (e.g., inside chocolate coating) must be declared if they perform a function in the final product

Three Types of Date Marking

FSSAI mandates date markings on all pre-packaged foods. Understanding the difference between them can prevent both food waste and genuine food safety risks.

📦 Best Before (BB)

The product is at its peak quality until this date. After it, the food may taste different, lose potency (vitamins), or change texture — but is usually still safe to consume if stored correctly and there's no visible spoilage.

Examples: Biscuits, chips, packaged spices, canned goods, pasta, cereals

⚠️ Use By (UB)

A safety date. Do NOT consume after this date — even if the food looks and smells normal. Dangerous pathogens can grow without visible signs. This is used for highly perishable foods.

Examples: Fresh milk, yoghurt, paneer, chilled ready meals, raw meat

🏭 Manufactured On (MFD)

The date of production. Mandatory alongside BB or UB on most products. Helps you calculate remaining shelf life and compare freshness between batches.

Always required; used to calculate shelf life from production date

How to Read Date Formats

  • DD/MM/YYYY — Standard Indian format: day first, then month, then year
  • MM/YYYY — Month and year only (common for products with >3 month shelf life)
  • MMM YYYY — e.g., "JAN 2026" — month abbreviation + year
  • Julian dates — Some manufacturers use day-of-year codes (e.g., "026" = Jan 26). Look for an explanation key on the pack or outer carton
  • Lot/Batch codes — e.g., "L24085A" — these are traceability codes, not date codes (though they often encode the production date)
🔍 Tip: Date markings must be printed, embossed or laser-marked directly on the pack — not on a sticker that can be removed and replaced. A product with a peel-off date label is suspect. Report such products to FSSAI.

What to Do After Each Date Type

  • After Best Before: Use your senses — smell, look, taste cautiously. Many products are still fine for days or weeks. Exceptions: baby food, infant formula (strict).
  • After Use By: Discard the product, even if it looks and smells fine. Do not taste-test.
  • Blurred or missing date: Do not purchase. Report to the retailer and FSSAI if sold openly.

What is the FSSAI Licence Number?

The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) is India's food regulatory body. Every food business operator (FBO) — manufacturer, packer, importer, or re-labeller — must be licensed under FSSAI. The licence number is a unique 14-digit number that must appear on every product label, usually alongside the FSSAI logo.

Format: The 14-digit number encodes the state code, product category, and registration type.
Example: 10019022001234 — The first two digits (10) represent the registration type; digits 3–4 (01) represent the state (Maharastra); remaining digits are sequential identifiers.

How to Verify an FSSAI Licence

  1. 1
    Go to FOSCOS Visit foscos.fssai.gov.in ↗ — FSSAI's official licensing and compliance portal.
  2. 2
    Select 'Licence/Registration Search' Look for the public licence search tool on the FOSCOS homepage.
  3. 3
    Enter the 14-digit licence number Type the number exactly as it appears on the product label, including leading zeros.
  4. 4
    Verify the details match Check that the business name, state, and product category match what's on the packaging. A mismatch is a red flag.
  5. 5
    Check licence status and expiry FSSAI licences must be renewed. An expired or suspended licence means the FBO is currently non-compliant.

What to Do If the Licence is Missing or Fake

  • Missing FSSAI number: The product is non-compliant with FSSAI regulations. Do not purchase. Report to the retailer.
  • Fake or unverifiable number: Report to FSSAI via the consumer helpline 1800-112-100 (toll-free) or via the FSSAI website.
  • Sticker over original number: A strong indicator of tampering or relabelling — report immediately.
  • Loose/unpackaged food: Street vendors and small eateries may operate under a simpler registration system (not a licence) — but they still need FSSAI registration.
✅
The FSSAI logo alone isn't enough. Any food producer can print the FSSAI logo — what matters is the 14-digit number beneath it, which must be verifiable on the FOSCOS portal. A logo without a valid, searchable number is meaningless.

Understanding Health Claims on Packaging

Food manufacturers use health claims to attract health-conscious consumers. In India, FSSAI's Food Safety and Standards (Advertising and Claims) Regulations 2018 govern what can and cannot be said on a label or in an advertisement. Not all claims carry equal regulatory weight.

Common Claims and What They Actually Mean

🔴 "Low Fat"
Regulated

FSSAI allows "low fat" only if the product contains ≤3 g of total fat per 100 g (or ≤1.5 g per 100 ml for liquids). Check the nutrition table — this claim is often used on products high in sugar or sodium.

💪 "High Protein"
Regulated

Requires ≥12 g protein per 100 g (or ≥6 g/100 kcal). Verify the actual protein per serving in the nutrition panel — many protein-branded products barely meet the threshold.

🍬 "Sugar Free"
Regulated

Must contain ≤0.5 g of sugars per 100 g/ml. However, artificial sweeteners (sucralose, aspartame) may still be present. Also check for "no added sugar" — natural sugars from fruit can still be high.

🌿 "Natural"
Not well defined

No strict FSSAI definition exists. A product can label itself "natural" while containing preservatives and artificial colours. This is a marketing term, not a regulated health claim.

🌾 "Multigrain"
Not well defined

Simply means more than one grain — refined or whole. A "multigrain" biscuit can be made of 80% refined maida with tiny amounts of oat flour. Check the ingredient list to see how much whole grain is actually present.

❤️ "Heart Healthy" / "Good for heart"
Regulated (Nutrient Function)

Must meet specific nutrient criteria (e.g., low saturated fat, low sodium, no trans fat) AND carry a qualified disclaimer. FSSAI prohibits direct disease-reduction claims without approval.

🥗 "Zero Trans Fat"
Regulated

Allowed only if trans fat ≤0.2 g per 100 g. Since 2022, FSSAI has capped trans fat in all foods at 2% of total fat. Verify using the nutrition facts panel if trans fat is listed separately.

🧬 "Fortified" / "Rich in"
Regulated

"Rich in [nutrient]" requires ≥30% of the Recommended Daily Allowance (RDA) per serving. "Source of" requires ≥15% RDA. These are specific thresholds — check the % Daily Value column in the nutrition table.

⚠️
Golden rule: Ignore the front of the pack. Marketing claims are designed to attract attention. Always flip the product over and read the ingredient list and nutrition facts panel — they tell the real story.

What Must Appear on an Indian Food Label by Law

Under FSSAI Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations 2020, the following manufacturer-related information is mandatory on every pre-packaged food product sold in India:

  • Name and address of the manufacturer/packer: Full legal name and complete postal address including city, state, and PIN code. A P.O. box alone is insufficient.
  • Country of origin: "Made in India" or the relevant country of manufacture. Mandatory for all products. Imported goods repackaged in India must state both the country of manufacture and India as the country of repacking.
  • Importer's details (for imported foods): Name and address of the Indian importer/agent must appear on the label or on a sticker affixed before retail sale.
  • Customer care details: Since 2022, a consumer helpline number or email is recommended and increasingly required on many product categories.
  • Packer's information (if different from manufacturer): If a brand contracts manufacturing to a co-packer, both manufacturer and brand/packer details must appear.

Private Label and Contract Manufacturing

Many products sold under supermarket house brands (private labels) are manufactured by third-party factories. FSSAI requires:

  • "Manufactured by [Factory Name, Address]" — the actual manufacturer
  • "Marketed by [Brand Owner, Address]" — the company selling it
  • Both FSSAI licence numbers where applicable
Tip: If a label says only "Marketed by" with no manufacturer address, this is a compliance gap. You can look up the FSSAI licence number to trace the actual manufacturer.

Red Flags in Manufacturer Information

  • No physical address — only a website or phone number
  • Address that resolves to a residential flat or empty plot
  • Imported product with no Indian importer details
  • Foreign-sounding brand name with vague "India Office" address
  • Misspelled city or incorrect PIN code

How to Verify Manufacturer Legitimacy

  • Search the FSSAI FOSCOS portal with the licence number
  • Check the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) portal for company registration
  • For imports: check FSSAI's import clearance records via fssai.gov.in ↗
